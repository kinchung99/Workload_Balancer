The format, and why it scores

Three ingredients, cut against each other so the video never sits still for more than about twenty-five seconds.

IngredientShareWhat it is doing for the rubric
DRAMA~55sEngagement (2%) and the front half of Clarity (5%). Wordless or lightly narrated scenes of a student doing the exact thing the app fixes. The panel recognises themselves before you explain anything.
PRESENTER~95sDelivery and confidence (4%). You cannot score this with a voice-over alone — the marker needs to see a face, eye contact and energy. Four pieces to camera, top and tail included.

Longest single block is 30 seconds. That rhythm is deliberate — it is what stops a four-and-a-half minute video feeling like eight.

Who and where

Cast — three people is enough

  • PRESENTER. Your most confident speaker. On camera in scenes 2, 6, 12, 13 and in voice-over throughout. Same outfit all day — the four pieces get cut together.
  • STUDENT A. The drama lead. No dialogue at all, so cast whoever is most relaxed on camera, not the best talker.
  • FRIENDS B & C. Two extras, on screen for about eight seconds total. Any teammates.

Locations — four, all on campus

  • DESK / BEDROOM, night. Scenes 1, 9. Needs a lamp, a laptop, clutter.
  • LIBRARY or study room, day. Scenes 4, 7.
  • OUTDOOR or plain wall, day. Scenes 2, 6, 13 — all the presenter pieces. Pick somewhere quiet.
  • LAPTOP / desk, day. Scene 12. Scene 11 is a corridor or café.
Kit. A phone on a tripod shoots this fine in 1080p. What actually matters: the microphone — record narration separately, indoors, into phone earbuds or a cheap lav, never the on-camera mic outdoors. And daylight on the presenter's face: stand facing a window, or with the sun behind the camera.

Edit and performance

Assembly order

  1. Lay the full narration down first, end to end, and trim it to 4:20. This is your spine.
  2. Drop the four presenter pieces onto their lines, sync by waveform.
  3. Fill the gaps with demo capture. Speed screen recordings to 1.2× — UI always plays slower than it feels.
  4. Cut the drama in last, to the beat.
  5. Music: one instrumental bed, low. Lift it at 2:58 for the montage, drop it out completely under the final line.

Performance

  • Smile on the first line of scene 2. It is audible and it buys you the whole opening.
  • Eyes on the lens, never on your own preview. This is the single biggest tell of a nervous pitch.
  • Slow on numbers: "forty-seven percent", "eight days out", "three hundred and thirteen".
  • Land the jokes and move on. No pause for a laugh that isn't in the room.
  • Scene 13 is slower and quieter than everything before it. Conviction, not volume.
  • Fluffed a line? Re-say it cleanly and keep rolling. Fixing it in the edit costs eight seconds; re-shooting costs ten minutes.

If the cut lands over 4:50 — trim in this order

  1. Scene 11, friends (−12s) — the only scene nothing else depends on.
  2. Scene 1, cold open (−5s) — start on the blank document instead of the clock.
  3. Scene 10, drop Tonight (−10s) — keep Owing and Priority; they are the stronger pair.
  4. Scene 4 (−5s) — hold on A's face for two seconds instead of four.

Never cut: the dread drag (0:40), the five dials (1:16), the locked deadline (2:08), or "313 automated checks" (3:40). Those four carry Clarity, Novelty and Feasibility between them.

Five ways teams lose Presentation marks

  1. Running over. 5:01 is a scored penalty. Export, watch it, check the runtime.
  2. Explaining the ideation. That is marked from the README. Here it is a minute you cannot afford.
  3. Narrating the UI. "Now I click here…" — say what it means; let the screen show the click.
  4. Slides with no product. You have a live app and nearly half of this is it working. That is the advantage.
  5. Trailing off. End on the line and the held card. No "…so, yeah, thanks for watching."
