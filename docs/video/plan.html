The rubric — and exactly what full marks says

Presentation is 15% of the total. Green is the band this plan is built to land in.

The host's hint, quoted

"Your Ideation (25%) is judged solely from your README.md, not your video, which means you do not have to spend time in your video discussing all the ideas you had and why they were dropped. Spend the 5 minutes you have to explain your idea to the viewer, why it is novel, how it will be built, and why it deserves to be built. You will lose marks for Presentation (15%) if your video exceeds the 5 minute limit (as stated in the rubrics)."
What that buys us. Zero seconds on dropped ideas, alternatives, or process — that is a full minute back, and it goes straight into demo. Every scene below is tagged with which of the host's four jobs it does: IDEA · NOVEL · BUILT · WHY. Eight of the thirteen scenes are the product actually working.

Cast, locations, kit

Cast — three people is enough

  • PRESENTER. Your most confident speaker. On camera in scenes 2, 12, 13; in voice-over throughout. Same outfit all day.
  • STUDENT A. The drama lead. No dialogue at all — cast whoever is most relaxed on camera, not the best talker.
  • FRIENDS B & C. Two extras, about eight seconds of screen time.

Locations — four, all on campus

  • DESK / BEDROOM, night. Scenes 1, 8b, 13 end.
  • LIBRARY, day. Scene 8a.
  • OUTDOOR or plain wall, day. Scenes 2, 12, 13 — every presenter piece.
  • CORRIDOR / café. Scene 11 drama.
Kit. A phone on a tripod is fine in 1080p. What matters: the microphone — record narration separately, indoors, into earbuds or a cheap lav, never an on-camera mic outdoors. And daylight on the face: stand facing a window, or with the sun behind the camera.

Edit and performance

Assembly order

  1. Lay the full narration end to end and trim to 4:20. That is your spine.
  2. Drop the three presenter pieces onto their lines, synced by waveform.
  3. Fill with demo capture. Speed screen recordings to 1.2× — UI always plays slower than it feels.
  4. Cut the drama in last, to the beat.
  5. One instrumental bed, low. Lift it at 1:08 for the feature run; drop it out completely under the final line.

Performance — this is the 4% for Delivery

  • Smile on the first line of scene 2. It is audible, and it buys you the opening.
  • Eyes on the lens, never on your own preview. The single biggest tell of a nervous pitch.
  • Slow on numbers: "forty-seven percent", "eight days out", "three hundred and thirteen".
  • Land the jokes and move on. No pause for a laugh that isn't in the room.
  • Scene 13 is slower and quieter than everything before it. Conviction, not volume.
  • Fluffed a line? Re-say it cleanly and keep rolling — fixing it in the edit costs eight seconds.

If the cut lands over 4:50 — trim in this order

  1. Scene 9, Recovery (−6s) — drop the prescription half, keep the ledger and "a credit you're owed".
  2. Scene 1, cold open (−4s) — open on the blank document instead of the clock.
  3. Scene 8, the sticky-note drama (−5s) — go straight to the collision card on screen.
  4. Scene 11a (−4s) — one typing shot instead of the split screen.

Never cut: the dread drag (0:32) · the timetable flags (1:08) · "book my sittings for me" (1:34) · the friend planner (3:28) · "313 automated checks" (3:52). Those five carry Clarity, Novelty and Feasibility between them.

Five ways teams lose Presentation marks

  1. Running over. The host says it plainly: over five minutes loses marks. Export, watch, check the runtime.
  2. Explaining the ideation. Marked from the README. Here it is a minute you cannot afford.
  3. Narrating the UI. "Now I click here…" — say what it means; let the screen show the click.
  4. Slides with no product. You have a live app, and two thirds of this is it working. That is the advantage.
  5. Trailing off. End on the line and the held card. No "…so, yeah, thanks for watching."
